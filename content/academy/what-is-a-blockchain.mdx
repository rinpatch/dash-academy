Suppose two companies share a payment history. Your database says an invoice was paid; your partner's export says it wasn't. Which record wins?

A normal application has a ready answer: the database run by its administrator. That arrangement is useful. The administrator can correct a bad row or change the rules when the business changes. Yet everyone else must trust the same administrator not to erase a payment or quietly replace yesterday's records.

A blockchain solves that dispute by changing what counts as the authoritative record. Instead of one operator keeping the accepted copy, participants share a record that independent computers check for themselves.

## A log that grows at one end

A blockchain is an ordered, timestamped ledger of <Term id="transaction">transactions</Term>. It collects transactions into <Term id="block">blocks</Term>, then adds each new block after the last accepted one. The record is **append-only**: accepted entries aren't overwritten or deleted. If an entry needs correcting, the correction appears later in the log while the original remains part of its history.

You've used a structure like this in Git. Commits link backward through earlier history, so a sequence of them records how a project reached its current state. Rewriting an old commit changes its identifier and the identifiers of every commit built on top of it. You can't alter the middle and leave the rest of the history untouched.

Blocks have the same backward-linking shape. A cryptographic fingerprint called a **hash** links each one to the block before it, and the block also records a hash-based summary of its own transactions. Change an old transaction and that summary changes; the block after it no longer has the right link, nor does the history built after that.

That explains why an edit is visible, but it doesn't yet explain why anyone must care. After all, a Git repository owner can rewrite commits and force-push the result.

## Why there is no force-push button

Git hosting gives an owner administrative control over the copy everyone treats as official. A blockchain doesn't give that role to one operator. <Term id="node">Full nodes</Term> receive blocks from the network and independently check them against shared rules before accepting and relaying them.

An operator can edit a local copy, just as anyone can edit a file on their own computer. Publishing that copy doesn't make it the network's history. Other full nodes don't accept a block because an administrator says it is official; they reject blocks that fail their rules. In the baseline proof-of-work model, if more than one chain passes those checks, nodes follow the one that is most difficult to recreate. Current Dash applies additional acceptance rules, so greater accumulated work alone doesn't guarantee acceptance.

Hash links make a crude alteration easy to spot. But an attacker could build a self-consistent alternative in which every changed block points correctly to the changed block before it. Something else must make that replacement hard, or hashing would amount to a tamper alarm with an off switch.

## What makes a rewrite costly

Dash requires **proof of work** for its blocks. Proof of work is computational effort that must be completed before a proposed block can extend the chain. It supplies the cost that hash links alone don't provide.

To construct a competing history in the baseline model, an attacker has to redo the proof of work for the changed block and every descendant after it. Meanwhile, honest participants keep adding work to the history the network already accepts. The attacker isn't aiming at a fixed finish line; they must catch and overtake a chain that continues to grow.

The farther back the target sits, the more accumulated work must be recreated. A self-consistent alternative must still pass each full node's checks, and on current Dash, more work alone need not satisfy them. Changing one node's files is cheap. Recreating the accumulated proof of work behind a competing history is expensive.

Proof of work doesn't make a competing history mathematically impossible. It makes constructing one increasingly costly as later blocks accumulate; whether full nodes accept it remains a separate question governed by all of the network's rules.

## The bargain a builder makes

Removing the administrator changes what your users must trust. They no longer depend on your company to preserve the only authoritative copy of a shared record. Independent participants can check the same history under the same rules, even when they don't trust one another or the application operator.

That independence is useful when the record must outlive one service or when no participant should have the power to revise past events alone. It also takes a familiar tool away from you: administrative override.

If your application writes a mistaken entry, you can't silently patch or delete the accepted record. You append a correction. If you want to change the rules that nodes use to judge new blocks, deploying new code to your own server isn't enough; network participants must adopt compatible rules, or they risk following different chains.

The choice is therefore sharper than "blockchain or database." With your own database, users trust your administration and you keep the power to repair history unilaterally. With a blockchain, users can verify a shared history without relying on you as its keeper, while you give up the ability to edit that history or change its rules by yourself.

## What you accomplished

You can describe a blockchain as an ordered, append-only transaction log whose blocks point backward, much like Git commits. You can also explain where the analogy ends: a repository owner can force-push, while blockchain full nodes independently validate what they accept. Hash links expose changes; in the baseline proof-of-work model, constructing a competing history requires redoing accumulated work and catching a chain that keeps growing. Current Dash applies additional acceptance rules, so accumulated work alone doesn't guarantee acceptance. For a builder, the payoff is a shared record that doesn't depend on one administrator, and the cost is losing unilateral edits, deletions, and rule changes.
