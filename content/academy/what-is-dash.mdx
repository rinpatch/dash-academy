A customer pays at a shop. For the person behind the counter, the useful question isn't "Has a new block arrived?" It's "Can I hand over the purchase now?"

On Dash, the answer can arrive in about a second. The receiver gets assurance that the payment is safe to accept and can re-spend the received funds, even though the transaction hasn't entered a <Term id="block">block</Term> yet. The network automatically attempts this for qualifying transactions; the sender doesn't have to request a fast-payment mode.

That experience is the first job Dash was built for: digital cash that works at the pace of a checkout rather than the pace of block production.

## Two different moments

Payment assurance and block confirmation answer related but different questions.

The early assurance tells the shop that it can treat the payment as settled. Block confirmation comes later, when a block includes the transaction. Dash produces a block about every 2.6 minutes on average, so waiting for the next one would turn a quick exchange into a noticeable pause. "Average" matters here: neither the roughly one-second assurance nor the block interval is an exact deadline.

Picture the receiver's screen. First it reports that the payment is safe to accept, so the customer can leave with the item. Later it reports one block confirmation, then more as further blocks build on top. The second event doesn't retroactively make the first one useful; the seller already had the assurance needed to finish the sale.

Calling both events a block confirmation would hide what makes Dash feel like cash. The transaction can become usable before its first block confirmation.

That raises a harder question. If a block hasn't included the transaction yet, which part of the network supplies the earlier assurance?

## Why Dash has a second tier

An ordinary Dash full <Term id="node">node</Term> downloads the blockchain and checks blocks and transactions against the network's rules. That work lets each operator verify the Dash blockchain rather than trust someone else's copy. It doesn't provide Dash's additional fast-payment service, and ordinary nodes aren't compensated for taking on that role.

Dash therefore has a second network tier made of **masternodes**. A masternode is a server that performs additional network services, including the service behind fast payment assurance, and receives network compensation for that work. Running a regular masternode requires committing 1,000 DASH. Those funds must remain unspent while the masternode operates.

The committed funds and compensation distinguish a masternode role from an ordinary full node role, but the practical distinction matters more than the server label: they do different work. Full nodes validate the blockchain. Masternodes provide paid services beyond that validation.

Without the masternode tier, ordinary full nodes don't simply take over the fast-payment service; the receiver loses the experience described above. The feature that makes Dash useful as digital cash depends on the second tier existing.

Once Dash has paid servers doing extra work, another possibility opens. Those servers can support more than payments.

## The second job

Dash names its two jobs **Dash Core** and **Dash Platform**.

Dash Core is software for operating and interacting with the Dash blockchain, where DASH payments are recorded. It handles payments such as the one at the shop. If a feature's purpose is to send, receive, or inspect movement of DASH, it belongs to Core.

Dash Platform sits on top as an application-data layer. It stores and answers queries about shared data for applications, without making one hosted backend the sole keeper of that data. Platform isn't another payment network with another coin; it's a second workload on Dash.

The masternode tier also shows how the two jobs fit together. Regular masternodes, which must keep 1,000 DASH unspent while operating, serve Core only. **Evolution masternodes**, usually called **evonodes**, must keep 4,000 DASH unspent while operating and host both Core and Platform. Ordinary nodes can't replace the Core services of regular masternodes, and regular masternodes don't replace the evonodes that host Platform.

So "one network, two jobs" isn't only a tidy description. Both of Dash's distinguishing jobs rely on the second tier: masternodes support Core's fast payment experience, while evonodes carry that Core role and host Platform's application-data service.

## Choose by the feature's job

Don't choose between Core and Platform by asking whether a feature sounds "blockchain-like." Ask what the feature must do.

| Feature requirement | Where it belongs | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Let a customer pay 0.2 DASH at checkout | Dash Core | The feature moves DASH. |
| Show whether a DASH payment has been received | Dash Core | The feature reads payment activity from the Dash blockchain. |
| Store a delivery status that several apps can query | Dash Platform | The feature stores and queries shared application data. |
| Take payment and keep a shared order status | Both | Core moves DASH; Platform handles the application's shared data. |

That last case is common. An application doesn't have to pledge loyalty to one side. Split the requirement at its natural boundary: money goes through Core, while shared application state goes through Platform.

When the wording gets fuzzy, reduce it to two verbs. **Move DASH** points to Core. **Store or query shared application data** points to Platform. A feature that does both needs both jobs.

## What you accomplished

You can describe a Dash payment from the receiver's side: usable assurance can arrive in about a second, before the transaction's later inclusion in a block. You can also distinguish the network roles behind that experience. Ordinary full nodes validate the blockchain, while compensated masternodes backed by committed DASH provide additional Core services; evonodes also host Platform. Given a feature request, you can now put DASH movement in Core, shared application data in Platform, or split a feature across both.
