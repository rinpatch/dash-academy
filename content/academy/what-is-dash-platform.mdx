Last lesson ended on a promise: one network, two jobs. Dash Core was the first job — money that settles in about a second. This lesson is the second job: Dash Platform, the layer where your application's data lives. By the end you will be able to point at a feature and say whether it is a Platform job, name a few things already built on it, and know the exact app you will assemble across the rest of this course.

## A database you don't run

Here is the version of this story for someone who builds web apps. Every app you write has two pieces: the code, and a database that remembers its users' state. Dash takes that second piece and moves it onto the network. Platform is a decentralized data layer — a place to store your application's structured data where no single company runs the servers.

The difference is *how* you describe your data. On a normal stack you might write table schemas, migrations, and triggers. On Dash you publish one thing: a **data contract**. A data contract is a JSON Schema that declares the document types your app uses — their fields, their types, and the indexes you want to query by. It plays the same role a schema does for a document-oriented database like MongoDB. There is no program to deploy and no function the network has to run. You describe the shape of your data, and the network stores and checks it.

Why does that matter? Because most security bugs in a decentralized app live in code its author wrote — the auth check, the transfer function, the owner-mint loop. When all you hand the network is a schema, there is none of that to get wrong. The network validates every write against the contract and rejects anything that does not fit. Your job shrinks to declaring what a valid record looks like.

## Documents, and who may change them

If the contract is the schema, the actual data is a set of **documents** — JSON records that conform to it. A document is the unit this whole course turns on: a note, a profile, a username, an asset. Every document carries a little base furniture regardless of the app: a unique id, a type naming which part of the contract it matches, the id of its owner, and a revision number that ticks up each time it changes.

That owner field is the part that replaces your hiring a database administrator. Each document is owned by a Platform <Term id="identity">identity</Term> — a persistent account with its own keys. Changing a document means submitting a signed <Term id="state-transition">state transition</Term>, and only the owning identity can sign it. So ownership is not a column you remember to check inside your own code; the network enforces it before the write is accepted. "Who may change this record" is built in.

And because the data lives on the network rather than in your one database, any app that knows the contract can read it. There is no silo to export from, and no single backend that can go down and take your app with it.

## Where the data actually lives

Two pieces run the storage. <Term id="drive">Drive</Term> is the storage component: the current state of every application's data, kept on the <Term id="masternode">masternode</Term> network. <Term id="dapi">DAPI</Term> is the front door — a decentralized API you reach over <Term id="json-rpc">JSON-RPC</Term> or <Term id="grpc">gRPC</Term>. Your app talks to DAPI, DAPI talks to Drive, and you never run or maintain a node of your own.

The part worth pausing on: an answer can come back with a proof. A normal backend returns whatever it likes and you trust it. DAPI can attach a cryptographic <Term id="state-proof">state proof</Term> to a response, so your client checks the answer against the network's committed state instead of trusting the server that answered. That is what separates a database you pay someone to host from one you can actually call decentralized. Keep it as a filter for this whole course: when someone promises trustless, the real question is always "verified against what?", and here the answer is the whole network's committed state.

## Proof the model works

If the storage story still feels abstract, the first two contracts make it concrete. Dash Platform launched with two real products built on this exact schema-plus-documents model.

**DashPay** is the social wallet: usernames, contacts, and payment profiles. **DPNS** — the Dash Platform Name Service — is the contract that supplies those usernames, registering human-readable `.dash` names and tying each one to the identity that owns it. Your users can send to `alice.dash` instead of a bare address. Notice the trick: a username is not a special kind of object, it is just a document — a record in DPNS's schema that happens to point at an identity. That is the whole storage model wearing its real-world clothes.

## What this lets you build

Once you trust the pattern, the things it composes into form a short but wide list.

**Tokens.** On many networks an application token means writing and deploying a <Term id="smart-contract">smart contract</Term>. On Dash it is a declaration inside your data contract — a name, a supply, and the rules for minting, freezing, and burning. The network enforces them; there is no code of yours to audit.

**NFTs.** Dash has no separate NFT standard. An NFT is a document with a unique id that represents one distinct thing, and the protocol gives it the behaviors you want — transfer, purchase at a set price, mutation, deletion. The same model that stores a note also stores a collectible.

Both already exist as worked examples you can read and run: **DashMint Lab**, an NFT marketplace that walks through minting, transferring, pricing, purchasing, and burning; and **Dashnote**, a notes app demonstrating the full create-read-update-delete lifecycle against a real contract. These are not diagrams — they are the exact operations you will perform yourself over the coming lessons.

## What you will build here

So this course is not a lecture followed by a toy. It is one application, assembled in the order the platform itself requires:

1. **Connect** — install the SDK pointing at <Term id="testnet">testnet</Term>, and read a real response from the network.
2. **Prove who you are** — create a Platform identity, the owner everything else will belong to.
3. **Get a name** — register a `.dash` username through DPNS.
4. **Declare your data** — design and publish your own data contract.
5. **Fill it** — submit, query, transfer, and purchase documents inside that contract.
6. **Issue value** — declare and operate a token, and charge it for document operations.

Notice the shape: every later step depends on the one before it, because on Dash you cannot write a document before you have a contract, and you cannot have a contract before you have an identity to own it. That ordering is not a teaching choice; it is how the network works. By the last lesson you will have a running decentralized application, and this lesson is where you learn what that app is made of.

## What you accomplished

You can now explain Dash Platform as the application-data layer of the Dash network: data contracts declare the shape of your data, documents are the records validated against them and signed by an owning identity, and Drive plus DAPI store and serve it with answers your client can verify. You can name what the model has already produced — DashPay and DPNS usernames, declared tokens, document-based NFTs, and the DashMint Lab and Dashnote examples — and you know the exact app you will assemble next: connect, identity, username, contract, documents, then a token.
