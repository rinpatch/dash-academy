You are building a checkout. A customer taps **Pay**, and you have to answer the question every payment system exists to answer: when is it safe to hand over the goods? Following that one question is the fastest way to understand what a blockchain actually is, because a blockchain is an answer to it.

## The only hard problem in digital money

A digital coin is ultimately a number, and numbers copy for free. Nothing about the coin itself stops your customer from handing it to you and to the shop next door in the same breath. That is a **double spend**. It is the failure that makes digital cash worthless: a payment that can be un-paid was never a payment.

Card networks solve it with an authority. Visa keeps the ledger, decides which of the two charges is real, and your checkout believes Visa. That works, and it has a price. You are renting someone else's answer, on their terms, subject to their approval, their fees, and their chargeback rules.

A blockchain is what the answer looks like when nobody is in charge.

## One log, copied everywhere, that only grows

If you have worked with an append-only log, a write-ahead log, or a Git history, you already have the shape of it. A blockchain is a log of transactions that the network only ever appends to, written in batches called **blocks**.

Two properties turn that log into something you can trust with money:

- **Each block carries the hash of the block before it.** A hash is a short fixed-size fingerprint of a block's contents, so the blocks form a chain where every link depends on the one behind it. Change a transaction from last week and its block's fingerprint changes, which breaks the next block, and the next. Rewriting history means redoing everything built on top of it, so tampering does not stay hidden. That property has a name: the log is *tamper-evident*.
- **No single computer holds it.** Full copies live on many independent <Term id="node">nodes</Term> around the world, all following the same rules for deciding which transactions are valid and which block comes next. There is no administrator, no primary, and nobody to ask for permission.

Now the double spend has an answer that needs no authority. Both payments are broadcast to everyone, the network appends one of them to the log, and the other one refers to money that the log already records as gone. Every node rejects it by applying the same rule, with no company involved in the decision.

The catch is timing. That answer only settles once your transaction is in a block the network has built on. So "when is it safe to hand over the goods?" becomes "how many blocks do I wait?", and on most networks the honest reply is several minutes. Hold on to that question — the whole next lesson is about a network that refuses to accept the wait.

## What actually moves through the log

It is worth being precise about what a payment is, because there are no accounts here — no row with your name on it and a balance to increment.

Value is a chain of custody instead. A **transaction** consumes the outputs of earlier transactions and creates new outputs of its own, and each output records an amount plus the condition for spending it later. Your balance is simply the sum of the outputs you can currently unlock.

That makes the double spend precise too: it is two transactions trying to consume the same output. Each output may be spent exactly once, and the second attempt is refused.

What unlocks an output is a pair of numbers:

- A **private key** — a secret 256-bit number that signs a transaction and proves you are entitled to spend. Lose it and the money is unreachable for good: there is no reset link and no support desk.
- A **public key** — derived from the private key, and used by everyone else to check your signature. The derivation runs one way only: the public key gives away nothing about the private key it came from.

You hand out neither of those. You hand out an **address**: a short string derived from your public key, hashed and given a checksum so a typo is rejected rather than silently sending money nowhere. An address is safe on an invoice, a QR code, or a public web page, so anyone can pay you and nobody learns anything that would let them spend.

## So why not just use Visa?

It is a fair question, and now you can answer it. Visa already solves the double spend, and it solves it in a second rather than in blocks. What a blockchain gives you is the other half, and for someone shipping an app it is operational rather than philosophical:

- **There is no server for you to run or secure.** The network already stores and validates the data. You write application code and talk to the network; you do not carry a database, its replicas, or its backups.
- **There is no single point of failure.** Copies live on many independent machines. One of them going down — or one company going under — does not take your app's data with it.
- **There is nobody to pull the plug.** No account to freeze, no vendor to be deplatformed by, no policy change that quietly ends your product.
- **The network is your backend.** Your app keeps working as long as the network does, not as long as one provider stays interested in your market.

None of that is free. You give up the ability to edit history, the ability to hide data behind your own server, and the comfort of having somebody to call at 3am. Decentralization moves those tradeoffs onto you instead of onto a vendor, and in exchange the app belongs to its users rather than to a host.

So the choice is not "blockchain or Visa". It is whether you want an app nobody can switch off — and if you do, you inherit the wait for blocks.

## What you accomplished

You can now say what a blockchain is without hand-waving: an append-only, tamper-evident log copied across independent nodes, which exists to settle the one problem digital money has — two payments reaching for the same coin. You know what actually moves through it, why an address is safe to publish when a private key never is, and what you trade away in exchange for a backend nobody can switch off.

You also have an open question. Settling a payment this way means waiting for blocks, and a customer at a counter will not wait. The next lesson is about Dash, which sets out to close that gap.
